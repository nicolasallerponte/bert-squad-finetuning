\begin{abstract}
\noindent
We fine-tune BERT-base for question answering on SQuAD v1.1 on one NVIDIA A100 of FinisTerrae III
and apply the single-GPU optimizations of the course one at a time, profiling each of them.
Two epochs take 45.8 minutes in the FP32 baseline and 7.2 minutes once optimized, a 6.4\x{}
speedup, while F1 drops by one point (87.97 to 86.97).
\end{abstract}
\vspace{1em}
\end{@twocolumnfalse}
]

\section{Setup and method}

The model is \texttt{google-bert/bert-base-uncased} (109\,M parameters), trained for two epochs
with AdamW (learning rate $3\cdot10^{-5}$, 10\,\% linear warmup, then linear decay) on 88,492
windows of 384 tokens, with PyTorch 2.11 built for CUDA 12.8.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Throughput} is measured over 100 steps after a warmup of 30 steps (60 with
        \texttt{torch.compile}), between two \texttt{torch.cuda.synchronize()} calls.
  \item Each configuration runs \textbf{three times}; we report the median and the range.
  \item \texttt{torch.profiler} records five steps per configuration. We report the
        \emph{share} of GPU time per kernel group, which is comparable across configurations;
        absolute times are not, since the profiled steps are the first ones of each run.
  \item The baseline is true FP32 (TF32 disabled). Quality is measured with the official SQuAD
        exact match (EM) and F1 on the full validation set.
\end{itemize}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{One A100 per job}, as the deliverable requires: the training script uses a
        single GPU, so more GPUs would sit idle.
  \item \textbf{32 cores}, because FT3 rejects GPU jobs with any other count per A100; eight
        of them run the DataLoader workers.
  \item \textbf{64\,GB of memory}, a generous margin for the tokenized dataset held in memory
        and the eight worker processes.
  \item \textbf{Time limits} of two to three times the expected duration, so that a slow node
        does not kill a run, and always below 6\,h, which places the jobs in the
        \texttt{short} QoS, the one with the highest priority.
  \item \textbf{Tokenization runs in a CPU-only job} (8 processes, 0.5\,GB peak), so no GPU
        time is spent on it and it is not counted as training time.
  \item The three GPU jobs are independent, so they were submitted at once on three GPUs: the
        whole set of measurements took 53 minutes.
\end{itemize}

\section{Results}

\subsection{One optimization at a time}

The optimizations are applied cumulatively, in the order seen in class (Figure~\ref{fig:throughput},
Table~\ref{tab:steps}). The three repetitions agree within 1.5\,\%, except one baseline run 7\,\%
slower, which is why we report the median.

\centering

show the range and labels the speedup over the baseline.}
\label{fig:throughput}
\end{figure}

\begin{table}[H]
\centering\footnotesize
\setlength{\tabcolsep}{3.5pt}
\caption{Throughput (samples/s, median and range) and peak memory of each step.}
\label{tab:steps}
\begin{tabular}{@{}rlrrr@{}}

\midrule

1 & + workers, pinned & 64.4 [64.4, 65.2] & 1.00\x & 5.3\,GB \\
2 & + TF32 & 194.6 [194.6, 195.4] & 3.03\x & 5.3\,GB \\
3 & + BF16 & 289.0 [287.5, 291.5] & 4.49\x & 4.1\,GB \\
4 & + batch 32 & 342.2 [342.2, 347.7] & 5.32\x & 6.6\,GB \\
5 & + compile & 397.0 [396.1, 402.4] & 6.17\x & 6.0\,GB \\
6 & + fused AdamW & 430.1 [430.0, 436.2] & 6.69\x & 6.0\,GB \\

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}

\textbf{Input pipeline (step 1): no gain.} The dataset is tokenized beforehand and kept in
memory, so building a batch costs almost nothing next to a 249\,ms training step. The GPU never
waits for data, and workers and pinned memory have nothing to hide.

\textbf{Precision (steps 2 and 3): the largest gains.} TF32 alone triples throughput without
touching the model, because it runs the FP32 matrix products on the Tensor Cores (peak
19.5 to 156\,TFLOP/s). BF16 mixed precision adds another 1.5\x{}: it also halves the bytes
moved, and lowers peak memory from 5.3 to 4.1\,GB. Since BF16 keeps the FP32 exponent range,
no loss scaling is needed.

\textbf{Batch size (steps 4, 7 and 8): diminishing returns.} Larger batches feed larger matrices
to the Tensor Cores and spread the fixed cost of each step over more samples. The gain shrinks
quickly: +18\,\% for batch 32, +7\,\% for 64 and only +2\,\% for 128, which doubles the memory.
The final run therefore uses batch 64.

\textbf{Compilation and fused optimizer (steps 5 and 6).} \texttt{torch.compile} adds 16\,\%
and fused AdamW another 8\,\%. The profile explains why.

\subsection{Where the GPU time goes}

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{profile.png}
\caption{Share of GPU time per kernel group, from the profiler traces. Kernels launched inside
the optimizer step count as optimizer; memory copies stay below 0.5\,\%.}
\label{fig:profile}
\end{figure}

In FP32, matrix products take 82\,\% of the GPU time (Figure~\ref{fig:profile}). Once
they run on the Tensor Cores, their share falls to about 40\,\%, and kernels that were
negligible become visible: speeding up one part makes the rest weigh more, as Amdahl's law
predicts.

Each later optimization targets exactly that remainder. \texttt{torch.compile} fuses the
element-wise operations (GELU, dropout, residual additions, LayerNorm) into fewer kernels,
cutting their share from 34 to 23\,\%. The optimizer then accounts for 11\,\%, because the
standard AdamW launches many small kernels; the fused version brings it down to 4\,\%, and
larger batches amortize it further (1\,\% at batch 128).

In the final configuration, \textbf{attention is the largest cost (32\,\%)}. PyTorch selects the
memory-efficient attention kernel rather than FlashAttention, because BERT passes a padding
mask, and its backward pass alone takes 23\,\% of the GPU time. This is where the next
improvement would have to come from.

\textbf{Time improves 6.4\x{}, less than throughput (7.3\x).} The optimized run spends about
56\,s outside the steady state, mostly compiling: its curve in Figure~\ref{fig:loss} starts a
minute late. This fixed cost weighs more in a 7-minute run than it would in a longer one. Both
curves drop at the start of the second epoch, when the model sees the training data again.

\textbf{Quality is preserved.} The baseline matches the published BERT-base results (80.8 EM,
88.5 F1). The optimized run loses about one point, plausibly because batch 64 makes four times
fewer optimizer updates at the same learning rate; tuning it is outside the scope of this
deliverable.

\textbf{Utilization.} Counting 204\,GFLOP per training sample (Kaplan et al., 2020), the baseline
reaches 13\,TFLOP/s, 67\,\% of the FP32 peak, and the optimized run 96\,TFLOP/s, 31\,\% of the
BF16 peak: a lower fraction of a peak that is 16 times higher.

\section{Conclusions}

\begin{itemize}
  \item Precision is the decisive lever: it accounts for 4.5\x{} of the 7.3\x{} gain in
        throughput.
  \item Each optimization exposes the next bottleneck, and profiling after every step shows
        which one: now it is attention.
  \item The code was the easy part; most of the work was deploying it correctly on
        FinisTerrae III.
\end{itemize}
